# Read this first: what are we trying to build?

Imagine a small business office on a busy Monday. The receptionist is answering calls, a customer has sent a picture of a product, accounts is matching invoices, and the owner wants to know which orders are late. The office does not need one mysterious machine that claims to run the company. It needs a way to handle **particular pieces of work** reliably.

A digital employee is a **named set of instructions, information, and permitted actions**. It may be able to read and write, and it may use other services to listen to speech, examine images, or update business tools. It should have a clear start and finish for every job.

## Five familiar comparisons

| Business idea | Digital equivalent | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Job description | Written instructions and boundaries | Stops one assistant trying to do everything |
| Filing cabinet | Approved documents and records | Gives answers based on company facts |
| Telephone and office software | Connected tools | Lets a reply become an actual task or record |
| Supervisor | Human owner and approval rules | Keeps costly and sensitive choices accountable |
| Job ticket | Task register | Makes it possible to see what is open or complete |

## A short example

A customer emails: "Can I change the delivery address for order 481?"

A well-designed assistant **does not immediately change the address**. It finds the order through an approved connection, checks whether address changes are permitted at this stage, and requests the proper verification or approval. If the order has already shipped, it opens a case for a person rather than promising a change.

The difference is simple: talking about a change is not the same as having the authority and ability to make it.

## Start with the work, not with a brand name

Before comparing tools, answer four questions:

1. What request arrives again and again?
2. Where does the true answer live?
3. What action, if any, must follow?
4. What could go wrong, and who would notice?

If you cannot answer these, adding more models and connections will probably increase confusion.

## Three kinds of system

**A writing helper:** You paste a request, receive a draft, and do the rest yourself. Useful, but not a workforce.

**A connected assistant:** It can consult allowed sources and prepare or perform a particular action in another app. Its usefulness depends on the permissions and reliability of that connection.

**A coordinated team:** Several specialists handle different parts of one task, with a shared record, a handoff, and an owner. More powerful, but also more to manage.

Do not jump to the third kind unless the first two have demonstrated value.

## What 'no code' honestly means

You should not need to write software to follow the examples. You may still need to create accounts, choose permissions, connect apps, select fields from dropdown menus, build visual flows, and test failures. Some company systems have no ready-made connection; those cases may require a specialist or a manual step.

## A useful way to explain the idea to your team

Consider the difference between a new office employee with a notebook and a fully supported member of staff. The notebook can hold notes, but it cannot check stock, open an order, or obtain spending approval. In the same way, a conversation tool becomes useful business infrastructure only when it is connected to accurate information, given narrow responsibilities, and supervised by people who understand the work.

Do not start by asking, “How many digital employees should we buy?” Ask, “Which requests spend the longest time waiting, what information do employees consult, and what is the proof of a finished job?” Then trace one real task. For an appointment request, the evidence is the live calendar and the completed event. For an invoice, the evidence is a matched payable record, not a beautifully formatted summary. For a delivery complaint, it is a case with an owner and a verifiable outcome.

### The owner's first conversation with staff

Ask five practical questions in a 20-minute meeting: Which requests come in repeatedly? Which mistake would upset a customer most? Which source of information is regularly outdated? What action should never happen without a person? And what would make the pilot easier for staff rather than adding another inbox? Write the answers down before choosing a platform.

> **Working principle:** Start with a business problem that already has a clear owner. Give the system a limited part of that process. Measure the result. Expand only when the result deserves trust.
